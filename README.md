# Boot

A company management game for Android and macOS. You start solo in a cramped apartment and build a company in one of six lanes: **Games, Websites, Apps, Social Media, Hardware or AI**. Each lane plays like a different management game. Mid-game, the **Pivot Point** offers you a second lane: expand into it, pivot into it, or (on Android) turn it down and stay focused. Lane pairings unlock crossover products and their own problems. Eras, rivals and a reputation that follows you everywhere shape the run, and the ending (IPO, acquisition, staying indie, bankruptcy or getting fired by your own board) reflects how you got there.

The full design is in [docs/DESIGN.md](docs/DESIGN.md).

**New in 1.3 (Android):** esports. Research esports and your competitive hits (action or strategy games that scored 7.5+, on an engine with multiplayer) can get a pro league. Sponsors pay for every viewer, watching keeps people buying and playing, and twice a year there's a championship with a prize pool you choose: big pools win big audiences, but only pay off on big games. Watch for match-fixing. Two new achievements, and stat tiles now shrink long numbers to fit instead of cutting them off.

**New in 3.16 (Android):** making a game is hands-on from the first idea to long after launch.

*Designing it.* The designer's summary has two new choices. Production: lean (70% of the budget, 15% less work, and it shows), standard, or lavish (half as much again, 15% more work, and every corner polished). And a hook, the one thing the game is known for: an open world, co-op, a story, roguelike runs, crafting, competitive play, mod support or cozy. A hook that suits the genre lifts the score and gets a line in the reviews. A forced one feels tacked on.

*While it's being made.* Three milestones each bring a decision:
- At the vertical slice: lock the vision, add a feature, cut scope, or keep to the plan.
- At alpha: fix bugs now, crunch for a month, or stay the course.
- At beta: an open beta (free; it builds buzz, and builds sometimes leak), a closed beta (costs money, finds more bugs, quietly), or ship it as it is.

What you decide shows in the score breakdown afterwards.

*Seeing progress.* The project card has a build timeline with the three milestones marked on it, the forecast score week by week (with how far it's moved in a month), what the team added this week, and the decisions made so far.

*After launch.* Port a game you made to a platform it isn't on yet; it comes out weeks later and a new crowd buys it, if it's good. Or put it on sale: half price for a week, more copies, a little more money and new fans, once a season.

**New in 3.15 (Android):** a social feed, and far more variety in everything the game writes.

*The feed.* The News tab has a new page: Chirp (the message boards, in runs set before 2006). Players, fans, streamers, journalists, insiders, rival developers, meme accounts and your own account post about every moment: your announcements (how a trailer landed changes what they say), release dates, launch day (following the reviews), delays (the second one gets the memes), awards won and lost, buyouts and chart-toppers. In between they keep asking about your next game and talking about what's out. Posts show replies, reposts and likes, and a Trending row shows the month's hashtags. The front page puts the latest posts beside the lead story.

*More variety.* Many more lines everywhere:
- critics' clauses for every part of a game;
- pull quotes, and more outlets' sign-off lines;
- launch-review and rival-release headlines;
- stage-report press quotes and chat;
- readers' comments.

Like all the game's writing, it draws its own dice, so none of it changes what happens.

**New in 3.14 (Android):** buying a company is a deal now, the stages look like the real ones, and the box art looks like real boxes.

*Buying a studio.* Tap any studio to look it over: its recent games and series, and how its owners feel about selling (open to offers, needs convincing, or not for sale). Due diligence costs a fee and tells you what's under the hood: a gem in development, key people planning to walk, a worn-out team, or a studio past its best. Whatever's there comes with the studio either way. Then make an offer: the premium is yours to pick, and the odds of a yes move with it. A listed company can pay half in shares. The owners answer in two weeks: yes, a counter-offer, or no (and no more calls for six months). A big studio can draw a rival bidder into a bidding war, and the biggest deals go before the regulators. Once it's yours, decide how to run it: leave them be (all the profit), fold them in (their three best people join your team), or cut costs (more profit now, worse games and bad press later). You can change that once a year from the studio's page.

*Real stages.* Every show is held on a stage after the real one, drawn in code without anyone's logos or trophies. The Game Awards has its giant curved LED wall and catwalk, the Spike awards their rock-show flames, the BAFTAs a London theatre's red velvet, and the Golden Joysticks are all gold. D.I.C.E. is a Las Vegas ballroom, GDC a conference hall and E3 an expo hall, while Summer Game Fest is a festival stage at sunset. The console makers each have their own set (a white studio for a Nintendo Direct, PlayStation blue, Xbox green), and Gamescom's opening night is wrapped in screens.

*Box art.* Covers look more like real boxes: the hero is lit from behind with a shadow at their feet, there's a film grade and grain, and the publisher's mark sits on the platform band. Each genre gets its own lettering: gold serif for fantasy and adventure, brushed chrome for action, a glowing monospaced sign for sci-fi and cyberpunk, bone-and-blood for horror, and candy with a white outline for casual games. (Real games' actual covers can't be included: they belong to their publishers.)

**New in 3.13 (Android):** show night, and a better press.

*Award nights.* When a game of yours is nominated, you watch the ceremony on a lit stage: each category's nominees with their covers, then the envelope. Win, and you choose what to say at the microphone: thank the team (morale and experience), tease what's next (hype and wishlists for the game you're making) or thank the fans (more of them). It covers D.I.C.E., GDC, BAFTA, the Golden Joysticks and the year-end show with Game of the Year.

*Every stage reports back.* A console maker's showcase, a world premiere and your own stream now report like the trade show does: how many watched, the hype, wishlists and fans it brought, a crowd meter from cold to on fire, two outlets' verdicts and what the chat said.

*New moments around the shows.* A big streamer asks for a preview build (a buggy one can crash live). Fans can be rallied for the prizes the public votes on. And a founder with a good name gets asked to present an award.

*Decisions have faces.* A decision about someone shows their portrait, level and morale; one about a show shows the stage.

*Better reviews.* Every critic has a pull quote in their own voice (Edge in one word, Zzap!64 shouting), and the best games earn the outlet's own seal: Editor's Choice, Eurogamer Essential, a Zzap!64 Sizzler or Gold Medal. Comments name the game now and then and back a point up with a second one. Players leave reviews too: a thumb, the hours they played and what they said.

*Better news.* Stories have a kicker (Review, Awards, Shows, Charts, Rumour, Delay), the game's cover, a quote from someone who matters, and from the late nineties on, the readers' comments. A launch makes the news with the critics' roundup and the line everyone quotes. There's a monthly charts story, and rumours the week before each show.

**New in 3.12 (Android):** the founder grows, and your team has stories.

*You level up.* Running the company earns experience: a little every week, more for every launch, most for a hit. Each level, up to 10, brings a skill point for a small skill tree in three branches, learned in order:

- **Craft:** Hands-on (your own work counts 20% more), Eye for design, Craftsmanship (everything you make is a notch better).
- **People:** Mentor (your people learn 20% faster), Steady hand (morale settles higher), Inspiring (people quit half as often, and stories end better).
- **Business:** Pitch (15% more hype on stage), Networker (the press is kinder), Dealmaker (4% more demand for everything).

At level 5 you pick a second trait to go with the one you started with. Your level is under You on the Team tab, and on your card in the office: tap "Level and skills".

*Staff stories.* Now and then someone on the team has a story, told in two or three decisions over a few months, alongside the poaching and raise requests:

- **Dream team:** two people who are brilliant together. Pair them up (15% more from each), and later they may want to lead together.
- **Name on the box:** a star on the game team wants credit. Give it, and the next game decides it: a hit makes them famous (make them creative director, or someone will try to poach them), a flop puts their name on it.
- **Running on empty:** someone staying later every night. Send them home on time, or let them run hot until they need a real break, or push through and risk losing them.
- **Under their wing:** a veteran takes a newcomer on, who learns twice as fast while the veteran does a bit less.

Stories under way, and the ones that changed things for good, are listed on the Team tab and on people's cards.

**New in 3.11 (Android):** wishlists and a release calendar. Once you announce a game, with a trailer at a show, a showcase or a premiere, fans start wishlisting it: every trailer brings a burst, and hype keeps more coming each week, faster once there's a date to count down to. On launch day they turn into sales, from 2% of them for a dud to 20% for a masterpiece. "Calendar" on your game opens the next six months as launch weeks, each marked with what it would do to your launch: the season for your genre, the holiday rush (late November to Christmas, +20%), the January slump (−10%), the shows, and the big rival releases you'd be launching into. Pick a week and set it as your release date. On the day, a finished game launches by itself (the clock keeps running while it polishes); if it isn't finished, you have to delay it, by four weeks or until it's done, and fans grumble and some wishlists drop off.

**New in 3.8 (Android):** the show season. The year is full of stages now, each named for its time:

- **Award shows.** Besides Game of the Year (decided at the Arkie Awards, then the Golden Joysticks, the Spike Video Game Awards and, from 2014, The Game Awards), the D.I.C.E. Awards, the Game Developers Choice Awards, the BAFTA Games Awards and the Golden Joysticks hand out prizes through the year: game design, technology, artistic achievement, best game, an audience award, a fan-voted Ultimate Game of the Year and best indie game. Each jury has its own taste (BAFTA loves a small game with a point of view, the Joysticks vote with their wallets), and the indie prizes keep the blockbusters out. A win brings fans, reputation and a sales bump, and they don't stop the clock.
- **Trailer reveals.** Every stage is a chance to show what you're making. The first trailer is the announcement and lands hardest (it makes the news); each one after adds less. A world premiere slot at the year-end awards or Gamescom costs real money and builds big hype, and a finished game can be shadow-dropped live on stage.
- **Console showcases.** Nintendo (Space World, then Nintendo Direct), Sony (its E3 press conference, then State of Play), Microsoft (its E3 press conference, then the Xbox Games Showcase) and Sega in the nineties invite promising games on their consoles. Show a trailer, take their marketing deal (they pay and give you more stage time, but fans on other platforms grumble), or shadow-drop a finished game. A weak-looking game gets passed over.
- **Keynotes.** With 250k fans the summer show offers you a keynote stage, and with 200k you can stream your own showcase every September. If there's not much to show, viewers say so.

Today lists what's coming up in the next eight weeks, the Studio's awards card is now a trophy list, and there are three new achievements: World Premiere, Out Now and Trophy Cabinet.

**New in 3.7 (Android):** the yearly trade show. Every summer the big games show opens (the Consumer Electronics Show in the early years, then E3, then Summer Game Fest) and you choose a small, medium or large booth, or skip it. Booths cost what they would have that year, and the main hall is kept for studios with 50k fans. The crowd judges what's on your booth: a promising game that's well along builds real hype, on-trend games and sequels to hits draw extra, a first-week prototype gets called too early to judge, a buggy demo can crash on the main stage, and a great game on a big booth can win Best in Show (and a new achievement). With nothing in development you show your back catalogue: fans come, but hype has nowhere to go. A report follows every show with the visitors, the hype and the new fans, and Today counts down the last eight weeks so you can time a game for it.

**New in 3.6 (Android):** sound and music. Every era has its own tune, composed by the game itself in a style of its time: chiptune for the home computers and the retro revival, FM synths for the console wars, house for the boom years, a minor-key downbeat for the busts and winters, lo-fi for the bootstrap years, and synthwave for VR, the metaverse and the cloud. The music crossfades when a new era arrives, stops when the game is out of sight, and stays quiet if another app was already playing. Sound effects mark the moments: a brass fanfare when a game ships, a ding for every critic's score that climbs with the number, a cheer for a hit and a sad trombone for a flop, a chime when a game is ready to ship or something needs a decision, bells for achievements, a jingle for a bigger office, a sparkle for research, a pop for a hire, a coin for money you spend, a buzz when the account goes red, and an ending sting. Buttons and tabs click softly. Set the music and effects volumes from "Sound and music" in the menu, or on the new game screen; there are no audio files, as every note is synthesised in code.

**New in 3.0 (Android):** rivals that fight back, deeper businesses and a lot more to decide.

- **Rivals fight back.** Each business's main rival keeps an eye on you: the more of their market you take, the angrier they get (calm, wary, hostile), and an angry rival makes moves. Copycat products, price wars, ad blitzes, raids on your best people, patent lawsuits, exclusive deals and smear campaigns all arrive as decisions, and most squeeze your demand for a few months whatever you choose. Fight a lawsuit and the verdict comes months later. You can strike first: undercut them, poach one of their stars or run attack ads, each of which makes them angrier. Push a rival weak enough and they retreat from the market for a year. Every business page now has a rivals card with their strength, their temper, what's squeezing you and your moves.
- **An inbox.** Opportunities wait on the office screen without stopping the clock, each with a deadline: interns, conference talks, white-label partnerships, a startup team for sale, angel investors, interviews, retainers and pitches. Let one lapse and it's a no.
- **People and crises.** Burnout (give them time off or ask them to push through), two people who can't work together, a star who wants equity, a petition to work from home, a side project that took off, a security hole (disclose it, or patch it quietly and hope), a customer's viral rant and a client going bust owing you money. Some choices come back: a quiet patch can leak months later, and someone told to push through may still quit.
- **Deeper businesses.** The agency gets retainers from happy clients (steady money for a steady slice of the team's week) and pitches for big-brand projects. The app studio gets a price level and six A/B experiments whose results nobody knows until they've run. The social platform gets a creator fund, a premium tier and house rules (open, balanced or strict). The device lab can discount or mark up the device on sale and book a livestream or keynote for the next launch. And a trained AI model now waits for your call: ship it, put it through six weeks of safety testing, or release the weights. GPUs can be reserved for a year at 40% off.

**New in 2.2 (Android):** game ratings, reworked. Scores used to be measured against the best game you'd ever made, on a curve so steep that anything short of it looked like a flop, while what reviewers expected rose every year. By year eight the average game scored 1.4. Now every game starts at 5.0 and each part of how it was made adds or takes off points: craft (your team's points for the work against what a decent team makes that year, rising 10% a year), the topic and genre, the audience and platforms, the focus split, the design and tech balance, bugs and polish, the engine, trends, retreads and rushed sequels, and a small nudge for a drop well below your recent games or a new best. Above 8 each extra point counts half, so a 10 needs everything right. The review screen shows the biggest reasons, and the game report shows every part, so you always know why a game scored what it did. Strong play now averages around 7, with 9s for the best work.

## Play it

### On Android

The Android game lives in [`android/`](android): a Kotlin port of the simulation plus a Jetpack Compose phone UI. It needs Android 8.0 (API 26) or later.

The look is calm and airy: white cards on a soft off-white page, sentence-case headings, plenty of space, one big number where it matters and colour kept for meaning (money up or down, each business, the thing to press), with bottom sheets for decisions. From [Material 3 Expressive](https://m3.material.io/blog/building-with-m3-expressive) it takes the expressive motion scheme (springs everywhere), lane badges that morph into each lane's Material shape when picked, buttons that change shape when pressed (the play button morphs as time runs), wavy progress for work in flight, a shape-morphing loading indicator for AI training runs, and emphasized type for the big numbers. Light and dark themes are both supported; in dark mode the office is on the night shift.

Open the `android/` folder in Android Studio and press Run, or from a terminal with the Android SDK installed (JDK 21):

```sh
cd android
./gradlew :app:installDebug          # build and install on a connected phone or emulator
./gradlew :app:assembleRelease       # app/build/outputs/apk/release/app-release.apk
./gradlew :core:test                 # simulation tests (plain JVM)
./gradlew :app:testDebugUnitTest     # UI tests on Robolectric, no emulator; screenshots land in app/build/screenshots
./gradlew :core:balance -Pruns=20    # autopilot balance report
```

The build uses Android Gradle Plugin 9, compileSdk 37 and Material 3 1.5 (beta), which is where the Expressive components ship. The release build is signed with the debug key so it installs out of the box; swap in your own signing config before publishing to Google Play. The game autosaves after every action and picks up where you left off.

The status bar at the top shows your company, the date, cash and weekly profit, research points, and fans (or reputation), next to the clock: speed, a one-week step and play/pause. The five tabs along the bottom are Office, Lanes, Research, Team and Company (overview, money and loans, combos, goals); News is in the ⋯ menu and under the office. Leaving the app pauses the game. Saves from 0.1 and 0.2 don't load in 0.3 or later; 0.3 and 0.4 saves carry over (they play on Normal).

### On a Mac

Requires macOS 14 and Xcode 15 or later.

```sh
swift run Boot              # launches the game window
scripts/make-app.sh         # or build dist/Boot.app to double-click
```

You can also open `Package.swift` in Xcode, pick the **Boot** scheme and press Run.

The game autosaves to `~/Library/Application Support/Boot/autosave.json`. Use **⌘↩** to advance a week, **⇧⌘↩** for a month, **⌥⌘↩** for a quarter.

### In a terminal (any platform)

```sh
swift run boot-cli                  # interactive game; type `help` for commands
swift run boot-cli auto games 7     # watch the autopilot play a Games run with seed 7
swift run boot-cli balance 20       # 20 autopilot games per starting lane, with outcome stats
```

## Project layout

```
android/
  core/             The simulation in Kotlin (a port of BootCore). Plain JVM; tests and balance runs need no device.
  app/              The Android game: Jetpack Compose, Material 3, autosave, Robolectric UI tests
Sources/
  BootCore/         The whole simulation. Foundation only; builds and tests on macOS and Linux.
    Lanes/          One file per lane: GamesStudio, WebAgency, AppStudio, SocialPlatform, HardwareLab, AILab
    GameState.swift The company: lanes, staff, cash, ledger, valuation
    Simulation.swift The weekly tick
    Actions.swift   Everything the player can do
    Pivot.swift     The Pivot Point, later expansion, the ecosystem company
    Combos.swift    The 15 crossover products and their problems
    Events.swift    Decisions that interrupt the calendar
    Venture.swift   Funding rounds and the board
    World.swift     Eras and rival studios
    Reputation.swift Scars that follow you across lanes
    Endings.swift   Endings and the story of your run
    Autopilot.swift A scripted player for balance runs and tests
  BootApp/          The SwiftUI macOS game
  BootCLI/          Terminal harness
Tests/BootCoreTests/
```

The simulation is deterministic per seed and the whole game state is serializable (`Codable` in Swift, kotlinx.serialization on Android), so saves, tests and balance runs are reproducible.

The Swift `BootCore` and the Kotlin `android/core` are two implementations of the same design and the same numbers. A rule change belongs in both; each side has its own test suite and autopilot balance run to check it.
